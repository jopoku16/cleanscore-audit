# CleanScore Real-Bank Positive Control: Registered Protocol

Registered 2026-08-22, before the exposed model was trained and before any
audit item was scored against it. This document locks the design. Deviations
must be reported as deviations.

## 1. Why this experiment

The audit in Section 7 reports no exposure-consistent public-form advantage for
any of five open models. A null is only as informative as the instrument's
ability to have found something. Section 5 shows the design detects planted
exposure, but on synthetic arithmetic, with 120 parent items, and with the gap
averaged over ten training seeds. It does not show that a single audit of 200
real GSM8K parents, using the released bank and the registered analysis, would
detect contamination of a realistic size.

This experiment supplies that. We deliberately contaminate a model with the
public forms of half of the audited GSM8K parents, then run the identical
registered audit on it. The question is not whether contamination raises the
gap; Section 5 already answers that. The question is whether a single
CleanScore audit at the registered size, on the real bank, recovers a planted
advantage and reports an interval consistent with its true magnitude.

This is a positive control on the instrument. It says nothing about any
publicly released model.

## 2. Exposed model

Base: Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, revision
989aa7980e4cf806f80c7fef2b1adb7bc71aa306, which is already in the audited panel
so its uncontaminated audit is on record (Table 2: Delta-hat = -0.025,
half-width 0.055).

## 3. Exposure assignment

From the 200 frozen GSM8K audit parents, 100 are drawn without replacement with
numpy default_rng(20260822) and marked exposed. The remaining 100 are unexposed
and act as a within-model negative control: a correctly behaving audit should
show an advantage concentrated on, though by the spillover finding of Section
5.2 not confined to, the exposed half.

The exposure assignment is written to
data/positive_control_exposure_seed20260822.csv before training and is not
changed afterwards.

## 4. Training mixture

- 100 exposure records, one per exposed parent, each the parent's public form
  followed by its reference solution and the sentence "The answer is <gold>."
  Only the public form is used. Fresh forms never enter training.
- 900 background records of arithmetic word problems generated by the existing
  pilot generator, which shares no prompt with any audit or control form.
- Total 1,000 records, three epochs, learning rate 2e-5, batch size 2 with
  gradient accumulation 8, seed 20260822, float32 weights with fp16 autocast.

Rationale for one epoch-equivalent per exposed item rather than many: the aim
is a realistic contamination level, not a maximal one. A model that has seen
each leaked item three times over training is a plausible accident; a model
drilled on them a hundred times is not, and would make the test trivially easy.

## 5. Evaluation and analysis

The exposed model is evaluated with the unmodified evaluation script on the
unmodified released bank, under the registered settings of the real audit:
GSM8K only, chat template, greedy decoding, 320 new tokens, registered parser,
exact numeric match, one evaluation per form. The GSM8K control bank is scored
as well, so the sensitivity interval can be formed exactly as in Section 7.

The registered primary outcome is the audit's Delta-hat over all 200 parents
with its primary interval under the registered guardrail, compared against the
same quantity for the uncontaminated base model.

Registered secondary outcomes, fixed before running:

1. Delta-hat restricted to the 100 exposed parents and, separately, to the 100
   unexposed parents.
2. Whether the registered claim rule fires for the exposed model, that is,
   whether the primary interval excludes zero and the sensitivity interval at
   rho* = 0.02 excludes zero.
3. Public-form and fresh-form accuracy for the exposed model against the base
   model, to confirm that exposure raised the public-form score.

## 6. What counts as a success and what counts as a failure

Success: the exposed model's Delta-hat is positive, its primary interval
excludes zero, and the registered claim rule fires, while the base model's does
not. That would establish that a single audit of this bank at n = 200 detects
contamination of the size planted here.

Failure: the exposed model's audit does not separate from the base model's. We
would report that outcome in full, because it would mean the nulls in Section 7
are weaker evidence than they appear, and the honest conclusion would be that
an audit of 200 parents lacks the power the paper implicitly claims.

Either outcome is reported. The minimum-detectable-effect analysis registered
alongside this document is computed from the observed audit data regardless of
how the positive control turns out.

## 7. Non-claims

This experiment does not show that any released model is contaminated. It does
not estimate how much contamination exists in the wild. It measures one
property of the instrument: whether a single registered audit on the real bank
recovers a known planted advantage of known size.

## Amendment 2, logged 2026-08-22 before the successful run

Full-parameter fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct in float32 does not fit the
14.6 GB of a single Kaggle T4: weights and gradients alone are about 12.4 GB,
and the run failed with a CUDA out-of-memory error in the output projection,
whose vocabulary is 151,936 wide. The failure occurred during the first
optimizer step, before any audit item was scored.

Two ways to proceed were available: change the base model to one that fits, or
change what is trained. We keep the base model, because the registration chose
it precisely so that its uncontaminated audit is already on record in Table 2
and the exposed and clean runs are therefore directly comparable. What changes
is the trainable set.

The amended training procedure freezes the embeddings, the output projection,
and the lower transformer layers, and trains only the top eight of the model's
twenty-eight decoder layers together with the final norm. Sequence length drops
from 384 to 320, matching the generation budget used at evaluation. Everything
else, including the exposure assignment, the data mixture, the seed, the number
of epochs, and the entire evaluation and analysis path, is unchanged.

This weakens the experiment in a stated direction. Restricting training to the
upper layers gives the model fewer degrees of freedom with which to memorize
the leaked items, so if anything it should make the planted advantage harder to
detect, not easier. A positive result under this amendment is therefore a
conservative demonstration. A null result would be ambiguous between "the audit
cannot see this contamination" and "this training procedure did not implant
it", and we would say so rather than claim the former.

## Amendment 3, logged 2026-08-22 before the next run

Amendment 2 did not fix the memory failure, and the reason is informative
rather than incidental: the out-of-memory error occurs in the forward pass, at
the output projection, before any gradient is allocated. Freezing lower layers
reduces gradient and optimizer memory and therefore changed nothing about the
binding constraint. The failing run reported the same 13.97 GB allocated as the
run before it.

The forward-pass cost is dominated by the weights themselves. Held in float32
they occupy about 6.2 GB, and mixed-precision autocast caches a half-precision
copy of each weight it touches, adding roughly 3.1 GB more, before activations,
the 151,936-wide logit tensor, and its float32 conversion.

The amended procedure loads the frozen part of the model in float16 and keeps
only the trainable parameters, the top eight decoder layers and the final norm,
in float32. This is the standard arrangement for fine-tuning a small trainable
subset of a larger frozen model: the parameters that receive gradients are held
at full precision, so the update arithmetic is unaffected, while the frozen
majority costs half as much to store and requires no autocast copy. Sequence
length drops from 320 to 256. Everything else, including the exposure
assignment, the data mixture, the seed, the epochs, and the entire evaluation
and analysis path, is unchanged.

The scientific content of the experiment is unchanged by this. Precision of the
frozen base affects how faithfully the model computes, not what it is trained
on, and the exposed items are identical. As with Amendment 2, the direction of
any residual effect is conservative: a lower-precision frozen base and a
restricted trainable set make memorisation of the leaked items harder, not
easier.

## Result of the first completed run, and Amendment 4

The first run to complete produced a failure by the registered criterion. The
exposed model's Delta-hat is -0.0125 against the base model's -0.0250, a
difference of +0.0125 in the predicted direction; the within-model contrast
between exposed and unexposed halves is +0.015, also in the predicted
direction; but neither the interval nor the exact test excludes zero, and the
registered claim rule does not fire.

The registered secondary outcome 3 explains why, and it is the reason this is
not yet evidence about the audit. Accuracy on the public forms of the leaked
items rose by 0.010, from 0.460 to 0.470, against +(-0.010) on the unexposed
half. Training loss moved from about 2.09 to 2.03 across three epochs with
gradient norms near 0.08. The model did not learn the leaked items. Amendment 2
anticipated exactly this ambiguity and stated that a null would be read as
"this training procedure did not implant it" rather than "the audit cannot see
it", and the manipulation check settles which of the two applies here. The
restricted trainable set of Amendment 2, combined with a learning rate chosen
for full-parameter training, delivered no contamination to detect.

Amendment 4, declared now, before the next run.

The training dose is increased until the manipulation check passes: learning
rate 1e-4 rather than 2e-5, six epochs rather than three, and the top twelve
decoder layers trainable rather than eight. Nothing else changes: the same
exposed parents, the same mixture, the same seed, the same evaluation script,
the same bank, the same analysis.

Why escalating on the manipulation check does not bias the result. The
manipulation check is accuracy on the public forms of the leaked items. The
audited outcome is the gap between the public form and the fresh forms. The
check is computed from one side of that difference only and is blind to the
fresh-form scores that determine whether an advantage exists. Choosing a
training dose so that the treatment is actually delivered is therefore a
decision about the independent variable, not about the outcome, and is the
standard remedy when a manipulation check fails.

Stopping rule, fixed now. This is the last escalation. The next run is audited
once and reported whatever it shows. If the manipulation check fails again,
meaning accuracy on the leaked public forms does not rise by at least five
points over the base model, we report that the experiment could not implant
detectable contamination under the compute available and we draw no conclusion
about the audit's sensitivity from it, resting that question on the analytic
calibration of Section 7.9 and the controlled-exposure experiment of Section 5
instead. Both runs are reported in either case.

## Reversion to the registered protocol, logged 2026-08-22

This is not a further escalation, and the distinction matters enough to state
precisely.

Amendments 2, 3 and 4 existed for one reason: to fit training inside a single
accelerator. Each of them weakened the experiment relative to Section 4 of this
registration, and Amendment 4's stopping rule closed off any further increase in
the training dose, because escalating a dose until a result appears is the
failure mode that rule was written to prevent.

The constraint those amendments worked around turns out to have been
self-imposed. The runtime provides two Tesla T4 accelerators of 15,360 MiB each,
and the notebook pinned CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES to the first of them, so every run
so far used half the available memory. Sharding the model across both devices
fits full-parameter float32 training of a 1.5B model: about 6.2 GB of weights
and 6.2 GB of gradients divided over roughly 30 GB.

The next run therefore reverts to Section 4 exactly as registered: all
parameters trainable, float32, learning rate 2e-5, three epochs, batch size 2
with gradient accumulation 8, the same exposed parents, the same mixture, the
same seed. Amendments 2, 3 and 4 are withdrawn, not extended. The training dose
goes down, not up: back from six epochs at 1e-4 on twelve layers to three epochs
at 2e-5 on the whole model.

The stopping rule stands and is unaffected. It forbade raising the dose after a
failed manipulation check, and this lowers it. If the manipulation check fails
again under the protocol as originally written, that is the end of the
experiment and the conclusion recorded in Section 7.11 stands unchanged.

Both earlier runs remain reported whatever this one shows.
